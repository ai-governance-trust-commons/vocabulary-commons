Ideas Worth Debating

Who can stop an automated decision? What might be lost by stopping it? These authors disagree about which risks matter, who should act, and what good oversight can achieve.

This selective English-language collection was assembled on 26 November 2026. Each entry gives the original publication date. “The idea” summarizes the author’s argument. “Worth questioning” introduces a Commons editorial question. Inclusion carries no endorsement or finding that the claims have been demonstrated.

Does having a human in charge mean someone is in control? Does AI require a different kind of governance? Where does safety actually reside? Who has power to inspect, contest, and change the system? Should governance do more than prevent harm?

Does having a human in charge mean someone is in control?

A signature, review step, or assigned responsibility doesn't necessarily tell us whether someone could understand the situation and change the outcome.

  • HIA-001 · Madeleine Clare Elish · 23 March 2019

    Moral Crumple Zones: Cautionary Tales in Human-Robot Interaction

    The idea · Elish examines accidents in which a person absorbed blame for decisions made across a larger automated system. The person held responsible could have had little power to change the outcome.

    Worth questioning · Which decisions did the human really have the ability to affect?

    Read the original ↗

  • HIA-011 · Phaedra Boinodiris and Jamie Mackenzie · IBM · 17 June 2026

    Why “human in the loop” alone is not a governance strategy

    The idea · A human approval step may provide reassurance without the knowledge, independence, or practical power necessary for meaningful oversight.

    Worth questioning · What would distinguish a genuine review from a rubber stamp?

    Read the original ↗

  • HIA-012 · Beth Stackpole · MIT Sloan · 22 September 2026

    A framework for determining when AI can make decisions

    The idea · Stackpole reports on a matrix developed by Ina Sebastian, Peter Weill, Thomas Haskamp, and Jan vom Brocke. It uses ambiguity and the consequences of error to distinguish decisions suitable for automation from those requiring human leadership.

    Worth questioning · Will that allocation still work when an agent's speed, scope, or downstream effects change?

    Read the original ↗

Does AI require a different kind of governance?

Some argue that extraordinary capabilities require extraordinary restrictions and inspection. Others argue that familiar technologies and institutions offer the better starting point.

  • HIA-004 · Future of Life Institute · 22 March 2023

    Pause Giant AI Experiments: An Open Letter

    The idea · The letter calls for a public, verifiable pause of at least six months in training systems more powerful than GPT-4. It proposes using that time to develop shared safety protocols and independent oversight.

    Worth questioning · Could a pause be defined, enforced, and evaluated in ways that actually change risk?

    Read the original ↗

  • HIA-005 · Sam Altman, Greg Brockman, and Ilya Sutskever · OpenAI · 22 May 2023

    Governance of superintelligence

    The idea · Proposes special international governance and inspection for a possible class of much more capable future AI systems.

    Worth questioning · How could oversight of model development govern consequences that arise across deployed organizations?

    Read the original ↗

  • HIA-009 · Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor · 15 April 2025

    AI as Normal Technology

    The idea · Argues that AI should be treated as a powerful technology shaped by human institutions, rather than as an inevitably independent species.

    Worth questioning · When does the ordinary-technology analogy clarify the problem, and when might it conceal genuinely new behavior?

    Read the original ↗

  • HIA-010 · Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan · 21 May 2026

    Do AI Risks Require Extraordinary Government Intervention?

    The idea · Challenges the assumption that unusual AI risks necessarily justify exceptional government intervention.

    Worth questioning · Which risks call for improved sector-level controls, and which expose a genuinely different control problem?

    Read the original ↗

  • HIA-013 · Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan · 14 September 2026

    The AI-as-Normal-Technology view of loss-of-control incidents

    The idea · Kapoor and Narayanan argue that recent incidents call for both better security practice and research into how to control increasingly capable agents. They also describe where their earlier expectations underestimated the risks.

    Worth questioning · What do the original incident records demonstrate—and what are we inferring from them?

    Read the original ↗

Where does safety actually reside?

One school of thought focuses on making and supervising models. Another says meaningful safety questions arise only when we look at the full system and its setting.

  • HIA-003 · Anthropic · 8 March 2023

    Core views on AI safety: When, why, what, and how

    The idea · Advocates empirical safety research and new methods of supervising increasingly capable AI, including AI-assisted oversight.

    Worth questioning · If AI helps supervise AI, how do we test the supervisor's independence and practical effectiveness?

    Read the original ↗

  • HIA-007 · Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor · 12 March 2024

    AI safety is not a model property

    The idea · Argues that risk depends on the specific deployment and environment, not simply on the model viewed in isolation.

    Worth questioning · What should be tested at the model level, and what must be tested in the complete operational system?

    Read the original ↗

Who has power to inspect, contest, and change the system?

Transparency and openness sound reassuring. But seeing how something works, being free to switch suppliers, and being able to intervene in its effects are different forms of power.

  • HIA-002 · Amba Kak and Sarah Myers West · AI Now Institute · 11 April 2023

    2023 Landscape: Confronting Tech Power

    The idea · Argues that audits and voluntary transparency may be inadequate unless concentration, incentives, and institutional power are addressed.

    Worth questioning · Could an oversight process be technically sound yet powerless because of who sets its terms?

    Read the original ↗

  • HIA-008 · Mark Zuckerberg · Meta · 23 July 2024

    Open Source AI is the Path Forward

    The idea · Writing as Meta’s CEO during a Llama release, Zuckerberg argues that open models give users more freedom to adapt systems and switch suppliers. Meta also has a business interest in wider adoption of its models.

    Worth questioning · Does an open model actually remove dependencies, or move them to hosting, tools, supply chains, and downstream integration?

    Read the original ↗

Should governance do more than prevent harm?

A solely defensive view of AI risks missing innovation, beneficial cooperation, productive disagreement, and the costs of overly rigid control.

  • HIA-006 · Marc Andreessen · 16 October 2023

    The Techno-Optimist Manifesto

    The idea · Argues that technological progress benefits human welfare and that overly restrictive precaution can carry serious costs.

    Worth questioning · Can we account for the benefits of experimentation without dismissing evidence of harmful outcomes?

    Read the original ↗

  • HIA-014 · Mohammad Hossein Jarrahi, Paavo Ritala, and Maysam Akbarin · California Management Review · 12 October 2026

    Governing AI Through Human-AI Symbiosis

    The idea · Proposes governance that also supports human expertise, productive disagreement, and constructive human–AI collaboration.

    Worth questioning · Can control arrangements unintentionally eliminate the independence and competence they were meant to protect?

    Read the original ↗
