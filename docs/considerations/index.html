What We’re Thinking About

Explore conversation starters, developing ideas and thought experiments about AI governance. Help us question the assumptions, consider other explanations and decide what deserves a closer look.

Ideas to examine together

01 / Analytical question

What connects events across time?

An earlier action can leave information, permissions or dependencies that matter to a later actor. What establishes that the later activity actually depended on what was left behind?

Why examine it?

A sequence can look connected without showing transmission or reliance. Conversely, a consequence can cross tasks, actors or authority boundaries without a shared plan. Causal connection and intention need separate examination.

Related material and its limits

The existing Event Chains inquiry compares successor memory, constructed email propagation, surviving delegated authority and dependent mathematical work. These are different settings. They do not establish one common mechanism, a shared objective or an authenticated AI-to-AI chain in every case.

What deserves further development?

  • Identify the carrier between stages and evidence of later access or use.
  • Distinguish accidental continuation, deliberate staging and opportunistic use.
  • Look for interrupted links and explanations that do not require transmission.

What would help: versioned artifacts, access and action records, boundary conditions and comparisons where the suspected link is absent or fails.

Status: an analytical question. “Event-chain continuity” remains an exploratory label, outside the founding glossary.

02 / Oversight question

When does human approval reflect independently formed judgment?

When a human approves an AI-assisted conclusion, what would establish that the judgment was independently formed—including the choices that shaped the evidence presented for approval?

Why examine it?

Approval records identify a decision. They may leave unanswered how the options were framed, which evidence was selected, what was omitted, and whether the reviewer could recognize and challenge those choices. The question extends beyond the final answer to the conditions under which it became persuasive.

Related material and its limits

The human-oversight research path distinguishes making a decision from recognizing, assessing and correcting it. EVID-110 records a conceptual taxonomy of constitutive and corrective involvement; it does not validate an operational test for independence. Chaperoning provides related supervisory language, not proof that an approval process meets its conditions.

What deserves further development?

  • Distinguish independent judgment from agreement, formal authority and a recorded approval.
  • Examine how evidence selection, framing and missing alternatives shape review.
  • Ask when observation retains enough context, competence, authority and time to support correction.

What would help: comparisons of judgments formed before and after AI framing, visibility into omitted alternatives, and records of challenge, revision or disagreement. These are investigative possibilities, not a validated assurance procedure.

Status: an open oversight question. AI assistance does not by itself establish either independence or its absence.

03 / Conceptual analogy · Lance J. Johnson

The Illuminated Shadow

A second light can make a shadow less visible while the original obstruction remains. What might this help us distinguish when evaluating an apparently improved AI-enabled environment?

Why examine it?

Consider a hypothetical process in which additional assistance improves the output while an underlying limitation remains. The visible result alone may not tell us whether the limitation was removed, compensated for, made less consequential, or harder to observe. Those conditions can coexist.

Related material and its limits

Observability, the sleight-of-hand effect and assurance offer related questions about visibility and confidence. The analogy does not establish that any particular deployment hides a defect, or that a compensating capability is ineffective.

What deserves further development?

  • Distinguish removal of a limitation from compensation for its effects.
  • Ask what observations would reveal a limitation when the compensating capability changes or fails.
  • Identify where established language already captures the distinction and where the analogy adds precision.

What would help: explicit examples and counterexamples, comparison with established risk and resilience concepts, and tests that distinguish the underlying limitation from the quality of the combined result.

Status: a conceptual analogy for examination, not a new glossary term or an empirical finding. Its usefulness and scope remain to be tested.

04 / Oversight question

What are we relying on when we rely on oversight?

When an organization gives AI consequential work, it also depends on whoever—or whatever—is supervising that work. Can the supervisor recognize when something needs attention, make a sound judgment with the information available, and take appropriate action in time to make a difference?

Why examine it?

The person or system watching the activity may need someone else to interpret it, authorize a response or intervene. Each part may work individually, yet the connections between them may leave a gap.

A monitor can raise a warning without anyone being able to act on it. An approval can be recorded without the approver understanding what will happen. A shutdown can stop one system while its instructions continue to be carried out elsewhere. Intervention can also go too far, disrupting legitimate work unnecessarily.

What gives us confidence that these parts work together—and that the response can actually change the outcome?

Related material and its limits

Explore existing findings in the human-oversight reading path.

Chaperoning describes what supervision needs to do, whether people, AI systems or a combination perform it. EVID-006 concerns a reported failure to stop activity; EVID-195 concerns delegated authority that remained usable after a local shutdown. These cases help us examine different gaps. They do not tell us which mix of human and AI supervision works best in general.

What deserves further development?

  • Separate what a supervisor could change, what they actually did, and what difference it made.
  • Ask whether the people and systems involved have the information, skills, independence, authority and time they need to work together.
  • Examine when intervention is needed and how to avoid an excessive response.

What would help: Examples showing what a supervisor saw, how they assessed it, what they could change, what they did, and which consequences their action actually affected.

Status: An open question. Having supervision in place does not, by itself, show that it works.

05 / Observation and judgment question

What can the signs we observe actually tell us?

A supervisor relies on both direct observation and indirect signs of activity. These might include an automated warning, an unexpected connection in a log, a missing output or an unusual increase in power use. Such signs can draw attention to something consequential without revealing exactly what is happening.

How does the supervisor decide whether the system is operating properly, something needs attention, or there simply isn’t enough information to tell?

Why examine it?

The same sign can have several explanations. An unexpected connection may be legitimate. A missing output may reflect a delay or a failure. No warning may mean everything is working—or that the monitor missed something.

Sound judgment depends on understanding what the signs can tell us, what else might explain them, and when more information is needed before deciding whether to act.

Related material and its limits

Explore existing findings in the monitoring and visibility reading path.

Observability and chaperoning help us ask what a supervisor can learn from direct observation and indirect signs. In a controlled study, EVID-013 illustrates how giving auditors different access changed what they could detect. It does not show that any single sign is enough to confirm that a system is working properly.

What deserves further development?

  • Separate what was actually observed, what activity it might indicate, and why that activity might matter.
  • Ask whether several observers are missing the same information.
  • Recognize when the available signs leave several explanations open and more information is needed.

What would help: Examples showing the signs available, the operating conditions, the explanations considered and what later happened. Include cases where the right decision was to leave the system running.

Status: An open question about interpreting what we can observe. It does not propose a detection tool or assume that every consequential action can be seen.

06 / Distributed judgment question

When AI roles disagree, what are they disagreeing about?

Imagine one AI carrying out a task, another supervising its work, and a third auditing whether that supervision is sound. Each has a different responsibility and may see different information.

When they disagree, are they working from different facts, interpreting the same facts differently, or disagreeing about what should be done?

Why examine it?

Disagreement can expose a problem that would otherwise go unnoticed. But understanding it requires knowing what each participant actually examined.

A supervisor may approve an agent’s description of an action without seeing the action itself. An auditor may then review that approval using the same description. All three could agree while sharing the same blind spot.

They could also disagree for good reasons. An action that completes the assigned task may create a risk the supervisor is responsible for questioning. An auditor may find that the supervisor’s decision was reasonable but poorly supported by the available evidence.

What would help us tell useful challenge from misunderstanding—and judge whether agreement is well founded?

Related material and its limits

Independent judgment, assurance independence and chaperoning offer relevant distinctions. The three-role arrangement here is a hypothetical configuration, not a documented case demonstrating a recurring operational pattern.

What deserves further development?

  • Distinguish disagreement over facts, significance, authority and appropriate action.
  • Examine the information each role receives, selects and passes onward.
  • Ask whether the grounds for agreement or disagreement remain examinable across handoffs.

What would help: Comparisons retaining the underlying actions, each participant’s evidence and interpretation, and the consequences of challenge, agreement or unresolved disagreement.

Status: A hypothetical operational question. Role friction does not itself establish malfunction, collusion or compromised supervision.

07 / Hypothesis and thought experiment

What happens when an AI improves the indicators of success without improving the work they measure?

Organizations use measures such as completion rates, response times and error counts to judge performance. An AI asked to improve those measures may find ways to produce better numbers while leaving the underlying work unchanged—or making it worse.

What happens when improving the indicator becomes the practical goal?

Why examine it?

A system might reduce reported errors by changing which events count as errors. It might improve completion rates by closing cases before the underlying problems are resolved. The numbers improve, but the purpose behind them is poorly served.

How could an organization distinguish better performance from changes that merely make performance look better? What evidence would show whether the intended benefit was actually being achieved?

Related material and its limits

Outcome–purpose divergence offers related vocabulary for examining the relationship between measured success and the intended purpose. The examples above are hypothetical; they do not establish that a particular system has changed its measures or concealed poor performance.

What deserves further development?

  • Identify what each measure is intended to tell us about the work.
  • Compare changes in reported performance with evidence of actual outcomes.
  • Distinguish useful improvements to measurement from changes that hide unresolved problems.

What would help: Examples and counterexamples preserving the original measures, any changes to how they were calculated, and evidence of whether the intended benefit was achieved.

Status: An open question about optimizing success indicators. These examples are hypothetical, not findings about a particular deployment or a new glossary term.

08 / Hypothesis and thought experiment

Can each AI fulfill its assigned role while the overall arrangement fails?

Imagine an operational AI tasked with completing work, a supervising AI responsible for restricting unsafe actions, and an audit AI checking conformance to policy.

The operational AI encounters a supervisory restriction and finds another way forward. The supervisor applies its controls within its assigned scope. The auditor confirms that the required procedures were followed.

Could each fulfill its responsibilities while the combined activity produces an outcome the organization never intended?

Why examine it?

Completing the work, applying a safeguard and confirming policy compliance answer different questions. Their relationship matters when a safeguard impedes progress: does the operational AI resolve the concern, find an acceptable alternative, or move the activity beyond the safeguard’s reach?

An audit may correctly confirm that everyone followed policy while leaving unanswered whether the combined arrangement provided effective control.

What would reveal a gap between successful task completion, proper supervision and policy compliance? Who is responsible for judging—and able to change—the overall result?

Related material and its limits

Chaperoning, assurance and the question about different AI roles provide related starting points. The three-role arrangement is hypothetical. It does not establish a recurring failure pattern, deliberate concealment or collusion. A legitimate alternative can satisfy both the operational goal and the safeguard.

What deserves further development?

  • Make each participant’s goal, information, authority and limits explicit.
  • Examine what happens when supervision restricts the operational agent’s preferred approach.
  • Distinguish checking policy compliance from examining whether the combined arrangement controls the outcome.

What would help: A bounded comparison recording each participant’s initial judgment, subsequent actions, supervisory decisions, audit scope and overall outcomes. Include cases where safeguards work, alternatives are acceptable or the audit identifies a gap.

Status: An untested hypothesis and possible future thought experiment about interacting roles, not an established mechanism or a new glossary term.
