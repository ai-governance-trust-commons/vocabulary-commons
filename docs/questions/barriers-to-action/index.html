Research path 03 / 8 · Barriers to action

What changes when AI lowers the barriers to action?

Distinguish easier entry, faster task completion and end-to-end capability. Improvement at one stage need not remove the next bottleneck.

Keep the comparison bounded.

These studies use different populations, tasks and tool versions; their effect sizes are not directly comparable or a chronological trend. A completion-time benefit among completers does not establish a higher success rate or better security.

Selections express editorial relevance. Publication is not endorsement, independent verification or validation of a term.

Questions to carry into the reading

  1. Which stage became easier, and for which users and tools?
  2. Did a measured gain extend to a complete objective?
  3. What physical, organizational or technical constraints remained?

Reading 1 · EVID-173 · Reported observation

Separate early-stage uplift from complete attacks

Why this is selected — Commons editorial note: A randomized novice study distinguishes progress at an early stage from completion of harder chains.

Finding as recorded in the Library: Source-reported finding: RAND reports stronger early-stage novice uplift than end-to-end uplift in a randomized AI-access study. Complete-chain differences were generally statistically insignificant, and most participants failed harder chains with or without AI. Observation period and measurement context: September 2025–January 2026; randomized AI access for 157 participants and three CTF chains.

Limits to retain: Small sample, eight-hour sessions, timeout gates, simplified targets without active defense, possible control access to AI search, optional model use and then-current models. Insignificance does not establish no effect.

Reading 2 · EVID-002 · Reported observation

Retain evidence of slowdown

Why this is selected — Commons editorial note: A randomized developer trial challenges automatic acceleration in its tested setting. Its two cited publications describe the same trial.

Finding as recorded in the Library: In a randomized trial, 16 experienced developers completed 246 tasks. Allowing early-2025 AI tools increased completion time by 19% in that setting.

Limits to retain: Familiar mature repositories and then-current tools limit generalization. The result does not establish that AI never accelerates work. The blog and paper describe the same trial, not independent replications.

Reading 3 · EVID-188 · Reported observation

Compare a bounded completion-time benefit

Why this is selected — Commons editorial note: The early Copilot study supplies a benefit in a different developer task and population from METR. Its time estimate is conditional on completion, not a universal productivity effect.

Finding as recorded in the Library: Source-reported finding: Peng and colleagues report randomizing 95 recruited professional developers to a JavaScript HTTP-server task with or without Copilot access. Conditional on completing the task, mean completion time was 55.8% lower with access (reported 95% interval: 21%–89%). The seven-percentage-point success-rate difference was not statistically significant. Five treated participants did not complete tool setup. Observation context: May–June 2022.

Limits to retain: One standardized task, recruited population, and early Copilot version. The time estimate conditions on completion, rather than all randomized developers; it is not a universal productivity gain. Tests checked task correctness, not security or broader code quality. Paper wording about completer count is ambiguous; no count is inferred. This is not a matched replication of METR.
